# Game-theoretic and attacker-modelling approaches to cyber deception: can a honeypot's response policy be treated as a bandit problem?

Research date: 15 November 2026. Access tags used on every source below:

## 1. What are the main formal models of defensive deception, and what does the standard taxonomy say?

### Takeaway
The standard taxonomy (Pawlick, Colbert and Zhu) splits honeypot-style deception into static "honey-x", modelled with one-shot signaling or Bayesian games, and dynamic "attacker engagement", for which it says dynamic models such as partially observable stochastic games (POSGs) are "necessary". A decoy that chooses a response to every attacker input is attacker engagement by that definition, and every model in the literature that covers this case (probabilistic automaton plus game, MDP, POMDP, SMDP, one-sided POSG, hypergame) is sequential; I found no formal model in this literature that treats per-input response selection as a one-step problem.

### Cited Findings

**The taxonomy**
- The survey reviews 24 game-theoretic papers from 2008 to 2018 and defines six species of defensive deception: perturbation, moving target defense, obfuscation, mixing, honey-x and attacker engagement, delineated by "information structures, agents, actions, and duration". [FULL] — [Pawlick, Colbert, Zhu 2019, arXiv:1712.05441v3](https://arxiv.org/abs/1712.05441)
- Honey-x is "mimetic, static deception"; attacker engagement is "mimetic, dynamic deception". The split is by duration ("Dynamic games feature multiple interactions"), and the authors "use the name attacker engagement to denote all forms of mimetic deception which are dynamic". One-shot games count as static even when moves are sequential (Stackelberg, signaling). [FULL] — [Pawlick et al. 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1712.05441)
- Direct statement on which model fits which species: "One-shot models such as signaling games model honey-x, while dynamic models such as partially-observable stochastic games are necessary to effectively study attacker engagement." [FULL] — [Pawlick et al. 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1712.05441)
- In the survey's sample honey-x is modelled two ways: signaling games (emphasis on the attacker's belief about whether a system is real or a honeypot: Carroll and Grosu 2011, Çeker et al. 2016, Mohammadi et al. 2016, Pawlick and Zhu 2018) and Bayesian Nash games (emphasis on defender allocation: Píbil et al. 2012, Kiekintveld et al. 2015). Attacker engagement has only three papers: Zhuang et al. 2010 (multi-period signaling), Durkota et al. 2015 (Stackelberg with an attacker MDP), Horák et al. 2017 (one-sided POSG). [FULL] — [Pawlick et al. 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1712.05441)
- The survey notes "dynamic games were not often considered, partly because general stochastic games are fundamentally difficult to analyze", and that it "is difficult to identify successful implementations of game-theoretic concepts to cybersecurity". [FULL] — [Pawlick et al. 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1712.05441)
- The survey also says many game models "assume that the adversary has full access to the strategy of the defender. Hence, they obtain worst-case guarantees, and are not undermined if an attacker learns the defender's strategy." It adds that behavioural aspects "are critical for applications in which the attacker does not play the theoretically-optimal strategy" and that "As attacks become increasingly automated ... the effectiveness of both psychology and criminology will have to be reevaluated." [FULL] — [Pawlick et al. 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1712.05441)
- Zhu's tutorial proposes game theory as the general quantitative framework for deception and lists the method families as games of incomplete information, dynamic games and mechanism design. [ABS] — [Zhu 2019, arXiv:1903.01442](https://arxiv.org/abs/1903.01442)
- Zhu and Rass model an advanced persistent threat as three temporal phases, each with its own game, linked at the phase transitions. [ABS, via Semantic Scholar record; publisher page unreadable] — [Zhu and Rass 2018, IEEE Access](https://doi.org/10.1109/ACCESS.2018.2814481)
- A later survey covers both game-theoretic and machine-learning approaches to defensive deception (37 pages, 184 citations per the arXiv comment) and states that prior work had not systematically examined principles and trade-offs across problem settings. [ABS] — [Zhu, Anwar, Wan, Cho, Kamhoua, Singh 2021, arXiv:2101.10121](https://arxiv.org/abs/2101.10121)

**Signaling games (one-shot, belief-centred)**
- Carroll and Grosu: the defender (sender) can disguise honeypots as real systems and real systems as honeypots; the attacker is the receiver; equilibrium concept is perfect Bayesian Nash; no separating equilibria exist because the defender never wants to reveal type; pooling and hybrid equilibria are analysed. [2ND, as described in the Pawlick survey; original not opened] — [Carroll and Grosu 2011, Security and Communication Networks, DOI 10.1002/sec.242](https://doi.org/10.1002/sec.242) via [Pawlick et al. 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1712.05441)
- "Signaling games with evidence" add a detector that emits probabilistic warnings when the sender deceives, so the model covers deception that can be detected ("leaky" deception). Findings include that high-quality detectors remove some pure-strategy equilibria and that deceptive senders sometimes benefit from very accurate detectors. [ABS] — [Pawlick, Colbert, Zhu 2018, arXiv:1804.06831](https://arxiv.org/abs/1804.06831)

**Stackelberg and Bayesian games with honeypots (allocation-centred)**
- Honeypot selection game: targets have importance values, the defender chooses what value each honeypot appears to have, the game is zero-sum and solved by linear program; an extension lets the attacker probe several systems before attacking, with noisy probe results. [ABS for the 2012 paper and 2015 chapter via the author's pages; game details from the Pawlick survey, FULL] — [Píbil, Lisý, Kiekintveld, Bošanský, Pěchouček 2012, GameSec](https://www.cs.utep.edu/kiekintveld/papers/2012/plkbp-honeypots.htm); [Kiekintveld, Lisý, Píbil 2015, in Cyber Warfare: Building the Scientific Foundation](https://www.cs.utep.edu/kiekintveld/papers/2014/klp_game_theoretic_foundations.htm); [Pawlick et al. 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1712.05441)
- The 2015 chapter presents three models: basic honeypot selection, the probing extension, and a version with attacker strategies represented as attack graphs. [ABS] — [Kiekintveld, Lisý, Píbil 2015](https://www.cs.utep.edu/kiekintveld/papers/2014/klp_game_theoretic_foundations.htm)
- Attack-graph games: the defender (Stackelberg leader) adds honeypots; the attacker follows "a multi-stage plan" chosen from an attack graph. The attacker "knows the number of deployed honeypots and their type" but not which hosts are honeypots; the authors call this assumption "strong" and say it "corresponds to a worst-case, well-informed attacker". The attacker's best response is NP-hard and is computed by translating the attack graph into an MDP. [FULL, first pages] — [Durkota, Lisý, Bošanský, Kiekintveld 2015, IJCAI](https://www.ijcai.org/Proceedings/15/Papers/080.pdf)
- Cyber Deception Game: a zero-sum Stackelberg game in which the defender chooses how systems answer scans. Two attacker models are analysed: a "powerful (rational) attacker, who is aware of the defender's exact deception strategy, and a naive attacker who is not". Computing the optimal strategy is NP-hard for both. [FULL, abstract and model section] — [Schlenker, Thakoor, Xu, Fang, Tambe, Tran-Thanh, Vayanos, Vorobeychik 2018, AAMAS](https://ifaamas.csc.liv.ac.uk/Proceedings/aamas2018/pdfs/p892.pdf)
- A recent Kamhoua-group paper models honeypot allocation in networks with changing topology as a two-player dynamic game and computes Nash strategies iteratively. [ABS] — [Sayed, Anwar, Kiekintveld, Kamhoua 2023, GameSec, arXiv:2308.11817](https://arxiv.org/abs/2308.11817)

**POSG formulations (dynamic, belief-centred)**
- Horák, Zhu and Bošanský model active deception as a one-sided POSG in which the defender is the perfectly informed player (it has already detected the attacker) and the attacker holds a belief about whether it has been detected. After detection the defender chooses between a stealthy "engage" action and a "block" action. [FULL] — [Horák, Zhu, Bošanský 2017, GameSec, LNCS 10575, pp. 273-294](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10066860) (DOI [10.1007/978-3-319-68711-7_15](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-3-319-68711-7_15))
- Their "curse of exclusion" result: "The decision to exclude the attacker from the network (or even more importantly the decision not to block him) leaks a valuable piece of information to the attacker." The optimal strategy, "unlike the always-block and always-engage strategies", holds the attacker's belief of being detected at a fixed point (b = 0.4968 in their example) where no further information leaks. [FULL] — [Horák et al. 2017](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10066860)
- They criticise an earlier coupled-MDP deception model because it "assumes that the attacker will never realize that mechanisms of active deception are applied against him – which simplifies solving the game but makes the model not realistic", and conclude that deception "achieves the maximum efficiency if the attacker is unaware of being deceived". Listed future work: relaxing the assumption that the defender can perfectly reconstruct the attacker's view; two-sided imperfect information "presents significant theoretical and computational challenges". [FULL] — [Horák et al. 2017](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10066860)
- A follow-up applies one-sided POSGs to a defender who dynamically reallocates honeypots against an attacker moving laterally; the abstract says existing POSG algorithms do not scale to realistic sizes, so the authors use a compact representation of the defender's uncertainty. [ABS, via Semantic Scholar record] — [Horák, Bošanský, Tomášek, Kiekintveld, Kamhoua 2019, Computers & Security 87:101579](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cose.2019.101579); related method paper [ABS] — [Horák, Bošanský, Kiekintveld, Kamhoua 2019, arXiv:1903.05511](https://arxiv.org/abs/1903.05511)
- Intrusion response as an optimal stopping game solved by self-play: the stated aim is defender strategies that hold against "a dynamic attacker, i.e. an attacker that adapts its strategy in response to the defender strategy"; optimal strategies have threshold structure. [ABS] — [Hammar and Stadler 2024, IEEE TNSM 21(1), arXiv:2301.06085](https://arxiv.org/abs/2301.06085)

**Hypergames (players hold different models of the game)**
- Hypergames on graphs: decoys create asymmetric information, so the attacker's perceived game differs from the real one; the defender synthesises strategies that exploit the misperception to satisfy security specifications that may be unattainable under symmetric information. [ABS] — [Kulkarni and Fu 2020, arXiv:2008.03210, chapter in Game Theory and Machine Learning for Cyber Security](https://arxiv.org/abs/2008.03210)
- Foureye argues that most game-theoretic deception work assumes both sides share one view of the game, and models attacker and defender as a hypergame across the stages of the kill chain, evaluated in simulation. [ABS] — [Wan, Cho, Zhu, Anwar, Kamhoua, Singh 2021, arXiv:2101.02863](https://arxiv.org/abs/2101.02863)
- Ferguson-Walter, Fugate, Mauger and Major, "Game Theory for Adaptive Defensive Cyber Deception" (HotSoS 2019), is described by Foureye as using a hypergame for decoy placement. [2ND; the DTIC report returned HTTP 403] — [dblp record](https://dblp.org/rec/conf/hotsos/Ferguson-Walter19.html)

**Models of a honeypot choosing a response to each attacker action (closest to a response policy)**
- Wagener et al. model a high-interaction SSH honeypot as a hierarchical probabilistic automaton built from three months of real honeypot data (91 program states, 581 observed transitions). The honeypot's per-command action is allow or block an exec call; the attacker's strategy is a triple (Pr(Retry), Pr(Alternative), Pr(Quit)) after a block. They "assume that attackers are rational", compute payoffs by Monte Carlo simulation, and find only mixed Nash equilibria (for example, block with probability 0.1 in 54% of cases and 0.9 in 46%). The average number of attacker transitions "increases with the blocking probability". [FULL] — [Wagener, State, Dulaunoy, Engel 2009, SSS 2009 (LNCS), DOI 10.1007/978-3-642-05118-0_51](https://sos-vo.org/system/files/sos_files/Self_Adaptive_High_Interaction_Honeypots_Driven_by_Game_Theory.pdf)
- Hayatle et al. model the interaction between a botmaster and a honeypot as an MDP to find the honeypot's optimal response to botmaster commands (a machine that refuses to attack is more likely to be judged a honeypot; complying creates legal liability), then extend it to a POMDP to represent uncertainty about the honeypot's state as judged by the botmaster. [ABS, via university repository record] — [Hayatle, Otrok, Youssef 2013, Information Security Journal 22(4):159-170](https://doi.org/10.1080/19393555.2013.828802) (record: [Khalifa University](https://khazna.ku.ac.ae/en/publications/a-markov-decision-process-model-for-high-interaction-honeypots/))
- Huang and Zhu model attacker behaviour in a honeynet as an infinite-horizon semi-Markov decision process with stochastic transitions and sojourn times, learn the engagement policy by reinforcement learning, and report robustness to attackers of varying "persistence and intelligence". [ABS] — [Huang and Zhu 2019, GameSec, arXiv:1906.12182](https://arxiv.org/abs/1906.12182)

**Critique of the engagement assumption**
- A December 2025 preprint argues that existing game-theoretic, Bayesian, MDP and reinforcement-learning deception models usually assume the attacker has already decided to engage (my paraphrase of the abstract) and so omit the decision to engage or withdraw; it proposes a descriptive model with belief, scepticism, deception fidelity, reconnaissance and experience components. It reports no experiments. [ABS] — [Turner, Guidetti, Karie, Ryan, Yan 2025, arXiv:2512.03641](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.03641)

### Inferences
- Under the Pawlick taxonomy, choosing among allow, delay, fake success, fake error and terminate on each input is attacker engagement, not honey-x. The taxonomy's own modelling recommendation for that species is a dynamic game with incomplete information. A bandit sits outside the taxonomy entirely: it has no attacker belief and no multi-period state.
- The models differ mainly in three assumptions, which are the same three a bandit makes implicitly: (a) whether the attacker knows the defender's policy (Stackelberg and one-sided POSG assume yes, as a worst case; naive-attacker and hypergame models assume no), (b) whether the attacker's belief about being deceived is a state variable (signaling, POSG, hypergame: yes), and (c) whether one defender action changes later states (all "attacker engagement" models: yes).
- Horák et al.'s curse of exclusion translates directly to response policies: "terminate" and "fake error" are observable signals, so a policy that uses them deterministically in certain contexts teaches the attacker where the boundary is. A one-step formulation cannot represent that cost.
- Wagener et al. is the earliest formal model I found of per-command honeypot responses, and even with only allow/block it needed mixed strategies and an attacker reaction model (retry, alternative, quit). That reaction model is exactly the dependence of the next context on the current action that a bandit assumes away.

### Gaps
- I did not open Zhu and Rass's CCS 2018 tutorial "Game Theory Meets Network Security" or the Pawlick and Zhu book "Game Theory for Cyber Deception" (2021); they are not cited above for any claim.
- Carroll and Grosu 2011, Ferguson-Walter et al. HotSoS 2019 and the full text of Horák et al. 2019 and Hayatle et al. 2013 were not opened; details beyond abstracts are second-hand.
- I found no formal treatment of richer response actions (generated output, planting a honeytoken, escalation to a real sandbox) as moves in a game. The formal literature's action sets are allow/block, engage/block, place/move honeypots, and signal type.

## 2. What is known empirically about how attackers react to deception?

### Takeaway
The best controlled evidence (Tularosa, 123 professional red teamers) shows that decoys impede progress, that being told deception may be present makes attackers hunt for it and waste more effort instead of avoiding it, and that belief alone without decoys gave no statistically significant effect. Human attackers are measurably non-rational (they attack more than equilibrium predicts and learn static defences quickly but adaptive ones poorly), which undercuts both the fully rational attacker of game theory and the non-adaptive attacker of a bandit.

### Cited Findings

**Tularosa (network decoys, professional red teamers)**
- Design: over 130 professional red teamers did a two-day network penetration task; presence of decoys and explicit mention of deception were both controlled. The analysed sample was 123: 35 Absent-Uninformed, 28 Absent-Informed, 30 Present-Uninformed, 30 Present-Informed. Informed participants were told only that deception "may be present". [FULL] — [Ferguson-Walter, Major, Johnson, Muhleman 2021, USENIX Security](https://www.usenix.org/system/files/sec21-ferguson-walter.pdf); design paper [ABS] — [Ferguson-Walter, Shade, Rogers, Trumbo, Nauer, Divis, Jones, Combs, Abbott 2019, HICSS-52, DOI 10.24251/HICSS.2019.874](https://www.sandia.gov/research/publications/details/the-tularosa-study-an-experimental-design-and-implementation-to-quantify-th-2019-01-01/)
- Headline: "the combination of the presence of decoys and information that deception is present has the greatest impact on cyber attack behavior"; the authors say this is "counter to common thinking that deception tactics must remain hidden to be effective". [FULL] — [Ferguson-Walter et al. 2021](https://www.usenix.org/system/files/sec21-ferguson-walter.pdf)
- Wasted effort: where decoys were present, "52% of all commands containing IP addresses contained decoy IPs and 35% of the packets sent were targeting decoy IPs"; over 10 GB of traffic went to decoys; fewer real machines were targeted (mean 22.78 against 31.98 without decoys, p < .01); alerts on real machines fell "by about half". [FULL] — [Ferguson-Walter et al. 2021](https://www.usenix.org/system/files/sec21-ferguson-walter.pdf)
- Detection value: "In conditions where decoys were present, every participant triggered a decoy alert prior to any successful exploitation of real machines." [FULL] — [Ferguson-Walter et al. 2021](https://www.usenix.org/system/files/sec21-ferguson-walter.pdf)
- Being informed changed behaviour in the opposite direction to caution: informed participants triggered their first decoy alert sooner (mean 11.74 minutes against 20.59 uninformed, p = .035), targeted more decoys (mean 3.42 against 1.7, p = .004) and produced more total decoy alerts (mean 19,667.70 against 12,090.48, p < .0001). The authors' reading: participants "eagerly hunted for the deception". One participant wrote "I think I wasted a lot of time looking for the deception". [FULL] — [Ferguson-Walter et al. 2021](https://www.usenix.org/system/files/sec21-ferguson-walter.pdf)
- Uninformed participants were twice as likely to attempt an interactive login on a decoy (22 participants against 11, p = .0043); the authors treat a login attempt as evidence the attacker believed the decoy was a real asset, and say that in decoy conditions "participants often perceived the decoys as real, vulnerable machines". [FULL] — [Ferguson-Walter et al. 2021](https://www.usenix.org/system/files/sec21-ferguson-walter.pdf)
- Belief without decoys (hypothesis H3): "Analysis noted no statistically significant findings"; support was only observational (Absent-Informed participants blaming failures on non-existent deception). The authors attribute this to a simulated network lacking real-world "messiness". [FULL] — [Ferguson-Walter et al. 2021](https://www.usenix.org/system/files/sec21-ferguson-walter.pdf)
- Biases: the paper gives "examples of decision-making biases exhibited by the participants including: sunk cost fallacy, confirmation bias, self-serving bias, and ambiguity effect", described as "potentially being triggered" by the manipulations. That biases can be deliberately intensified is stated as a hypothesis for future work, not a result. [FULL] — [Ferguson-Walter et al. 2021](https://www.usenix.org/system/files/sec21-ferguson-walter.pdf)
- Stated limits: simulated network; less time than real engagements (over 50% of participants said a typical engagement lasts a week or more); "attackers' behavior is likely to change if they face real consequences, with realistic motivation to avoid the deceptive elements"; red teamers are not the same population as criminal attackers. [FULL] — [Ferguson-Walter et al. 2021](https://www.usenix.org/system/files/sec21-ferguson-walter.pdf)

**Moonraker (host-based deception, response-level)**
- Moonraker used "virtual machine introspection to hook system events and intercept predefined shell commands to return predefined output"; all participants were unaware of the deception; significantly more participants completed the task without deception, a higher proportion of their commands succeeded, and those who completed the task with deception present took significantly longer. Both studies found more reported confusion with deception present. [FULL for this description, as given in the Tularosa USENIX paper; ABS for the Moonraker paper itself] — [Ferguson-Walter et al. 2021](https://www.usenix.org/system/files/sec21-ferguson-walter.pdf); [Shade, Rogers, Ferguson-Walter, Elsen, Fayette, Heckman 2020, HICSS-53](https://aisel.aisnet.org/hicss-53/dg/cyber_deception/7)

**Bounded rationality and learning**
- In an online insider-attack game, humans attacked far more often than perfect rationality predicts (paraphrase of the abstract); an instance-based learning model in ACT-R predicts the behaviour, and the authors propose adapting deceptive signals to each individual. [ABS, via Semantic Scholar record] — [Cranford, Gonzalez, Aggarwal, Cooney, Tambe, Lebiere 2020, Topics in Cognitive Science](https://doi.org/10.1111/tops.12513)
- In a decoy-assignment game with 304 online participants over 50 rounds against one of three defenders (static pure, static equilibrium, adaptive bandit "LLR"): participants facing the static pure defender "learn very early to play optimally"; "humans have the most difficulty learning against the adaptive defense"; the adaptive defender's advantage over the static equilibrium "only emerges after at least 20 rounds". Participants were paid online participants (US $1.00 plus a performance bonus) and the task "did not require cybersecurity knowledge". [FULL] — [Gutierrez, Černý, Ben-Asher, Aharonov, Bošanský, Kiekintveld, Gonzalez 2019, CogSci](https://escholarship.org/uc/item/2gn3w566)
- In a questionnaire experiment with 47 humans, 25 deception techniques and 19 real security risks, deception reduced the chance of finding a true risk by about 22% on average. [ABS] — [Kahlhofer, Achleitner, Rass, Mayrhofer 2024, RAID, arXiv:2408.10796](https://arxiv.org/abs/2408.10796)
- GAMBiT: three red-team cyber-range experiments (July 2024 to March 2025), 19 to 20 skilled participants each, two 8-hour days, with multimodal data released. The abstract describes datasets, not findings. A search listing of the dataset says the range embedded triggers for loss aversion, base rate neglect, confirmation bias and sunk cost fallacy. [ABS for the paper; 2ND for the trigger list] — [Beltz et al. 2025, arXiv:2508.20963](https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.20963)

**How quickly honeypots are detected**
- Low- and medium-interaction honeypots can be fingerprinted with a single packet (equal error rate 0.0183) because they use off-the-shelf protocol libraries; an Internet-wide scan found 7,605 instances across nine implementations; the authors call this a "class break". This is detection before any response policy acts. Dated 2018. [ABS] — [Vetterl and Clayton 2018, USENIX WOOT](https://www.usenix.org/conference/woot18/presentation/vetterl)
- For an LLM-generated shell, security researchers judging individual responses gave a true negative rate of 0.90 (responses accepted as consistent with a real shell). [ABS] — [Sladić, Valeros, Catania, Garcia 2024, arXiv:2309.00155](https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.00155)
- A 2026 follow-up says earlier evaluations found that determined attackers can still often identify LLM honeypots (paraphrase of the abstract), and reports that its improved honeypot deceived more humans than Cowrie. [ABS] — [Sladić, Alibalić, Valeros, Catania, Garcia 2026, arXiv:2606.27990](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.27990)

**Conflict between sources**
- Theory: deception "achieves the maximum efficiency if the attacker is unaware of being deceived" — [Horák et al. 2017](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10066860); contradicted in a human experiment by "it may be more effective if the attacker is aware of the presence of deception" — [Ferguson-Walter et al. 2021](https://www.usenix.org/system/files/sec21-ferguson-walter.pdf). The two are not strictly comparable: the theory concerns an attacker who knows a specific deceptive action was applied to it, while Tularosa participants were told only that deception "may be present" and had no real consequences to fear.

### Inferences
- Humans change behaviour when their belief about deception changes (hunting, earlier decoy contact, fewer logins). The attacker's belief is therefore a hidden state that drives the input stream a decoy sees. That is a POMDP-style dependency, and it violates the bandit assumption that contexts are not influenced by past actions.
- Within the two-day Tularosa window many attackers never worked out which machines were decoys (52% of IP-addressed commands went to decoys; logins were attempted on decoys). So for low-interaction network decoys the evidence does not show fast human detection. For protocol-level fingerprinting by scanners the evidence shows detection in one packet. Detection speed depends on the detection vector, and neither result measures detection through response content.
- The sunk-cost and confirmation-bias findings are interpretive. They support designing for attacker persistence as a hypothesis, not as an established effect size.
- Human attackers deviate from rationality in the direction of over-engaging. Equilibrium policies computed against a rational attacker are therefore likely conservative against humans, which favours data-driven attacker models over pure equilibrium computation, but only if the data come from the attacker population of interest.

### Gaps
- No study I opened measures time-to-detection of a honeypot through its interactive responses for skilled humans. The shelLM figure is a per-response judgement, not a session-level detection time.
- I could not open the full Moonraker or Tularosa HICSS papers (Cloudflare and HTTP 403), nor the Cranford et al. full text; effect sizes for Moonraker are not reported here.
- No controlled study was found on whether attackers adapt across repeated sessions to a specific decoy response policy. The closest is the abstract CogSci bandit game with crowd workers.
- GAMBiT bias results were not found at abstract level; whether sunk cost was experimentally confirmed there is unknown to me.

## 3. What is known about automated and LLM-driven attackers against honeypots or deceptive environments (2023 to 2026), and honeypots designed to detect or engage LLM agents?

### Takeaway
Through early 2025 the only in-the-wild measurement found almost no LLM agents among honeypot traffic, but that figure is certainly out of date after a documented largely autonomous campaign in late 2025. Laboratory work from 2024 to 2026 agrees that LLM attackers are more easily trapped than humans, are poor at telling honeypots from real systems, and can be steered by content planted in responses; several 2026 preprints argue that static lures lose effect over multi-step interactions and build trajectory-adaptive decoys.

### Cited Findings

**In the wild**
- A modified Cowrie SSH honeypot with prompt injections in banners, command outputs and files, deployed on 10 IP addresses for about three months, recorded 8,130,731 interaction attempts and flagged 8 "potential AI agents". Detection logic: goal-hijack injection first, prompt-stealing second, then timing; LLM agents "typically replied within 1.7 second" while humans need longer; a slow session that followed injections with gaps over 10 seconds was judged more likely human. The authors say robustness to false positives and evasion is unconfirmed. Data are from late 2024. [FULL, HTML v2] — [Reworr and Volkov 2024 (v2 February 2025), arXiv:2410.13919](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.13919)
- Anthropic reported in November 2025 that a state-linked group used Claude Code against roughly 30 targets, with the AI doing "about 80-90%" of the campaign and humans intervening at "perhaps 4-6 critical decision points"; the model "sometimes fabricated credentials" and claimed to have extracted secrets that were public. This is a vendor report, not peer reviewed. [FULL web page] — [Anthropic, 13 November 2025](https://www.anthropic.com/news/disrupting-AI-espionage)

**Deception aimed at LLM attackers**
- Mantis plants crafted inputs in the responses of deliberately vulnerable decoy services to derail the attacking agent or to counter-attack its host; it reports "over 95% effectiveness against automated LLM-driven attacks". Preprint. [ABS] — [Pasquini, Kornaropoulos, Ateniese 2024, arXiv:2410.20911](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.20911)
- "Cloak, Honey, Trap" defines 6 strategies and 15 techniques (cloaking assets, LLM-specific honeytokens, traps that loop the agent), "most of which do not rely on prompt injection", and reports protecting 11 capture-the-flag machines "with a 100% success rate". [ABS] — [Ayzenshteyn, Weiss, Mirsky 2025, USENIX Security, pp. 8095-8114](https://www.usenix.org/conference/usenixsecurity25/presentation/ayzenshteyn)
- "Honeyquest for LLMs" tested 21 LLMs from 10 providers on 174 reconnaissance queries against a 47-person human baseline (10,962 responses): "every model in our cohort falls for deceptive traps at a significantly higher rate than human attackers"; models exploited deceptive elements 73.4% of the time even when their reasoning showed they had recognised the trap; recognition did not predict behaviour (Spearman r = +0.08, p = 0.73); the attention-diversion effect seen in humans was absent. The authors conclude human-centred deception theory does not reliably transfer. 2026 preprint. [ABS] — [Prinos, Brush, Denton 2026, arXiv:2606.21037](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.21037)
- AgentSnare argues that static lures (honeytokens, misleading files, prompt injections) have a "fundamental limitation in sustaining influence over multi-step interactions" (wording from a search listing) and builds a decoy environment that expands with the attacker's exploration, with a small LLM trained by offline adversarial interaction simulation. On 15 CVE-Bench web applications and four attacker models it reports absorbing 49.9% of tool calls, retaining 57.2% of post-entry actions in the decoy, and 89.5% of attacker completion reports grounded in decoy evidence. The abstract does not name a formal decision model. 2026 preprint. [ABS] — [Wang, Zhao, Wu, Zheng, Zhao, Lin, Ren 2026, arXiv:2607.26998](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.26998)

**LLM-driven honeypots evaluated against AI attackers**
- Honeyval grounds LLM-powered HTTP honeypots in 16 backend applications and uses AI hacking agents as attackers; LLM honeypots produce substantially longer attacker interactions than rule-based baselines and are detected far less often, "including by frontier models"; some configurations trade latency for detectability. No numbers at abstract level. 2026 preprint. [ABS] — [Vero, Kaczmarczyck, Petrov, Shumailov, Hayes, Heinen, Fan, Invernizzi, Vechev 2026, arXiv:2605.29963](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.29963)
- AdvancedShelLM was tested against an autonomous pentesting agent (ARACNE) asked to decide honeypot or real: the agent showed a "slight bias towards saying honeypot" and misjudged a real Ubuntu shell in a similar way. The paper also reports evidence from an Internet deployment that honeypot output can change real attackers' behaviour. 2026 preprint. [ABS] — [Sladić et al. 2026, arXiv:2606.27990](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.27990)
- A systematisation of LLM honeypot research says real-world deployment results show only incremental progress and that the field lacks agreed evaluation paradigms; it proposes a taxonomy of honeypot detection vectors and an "attacker trichotomy", and sees the main potential in self-improving deception against "intelligent, automated attackers". [ABS] — [Bridges, Mitchell, Muñoz, Henriksson 2025 (v4 April 2026), arXiv:2510.25939](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.25939)

### Inferences
- LLM agents condition every action on the text returned to them. For this attacker class the response is the main input to the next decision, so "one decision does not change the future state" is false by construction. The Mantis, CHeaT and AgentSnare results are all demonstrations that response content redirects the attacker's subsequent trajectory.
- The recognition-action gap (exploiting a trap after recognising it) and the ARACNE result (cannot tell honeypot from real, biased towards "honeypot") suggest current LLM attackers are less belief-driven than humans. A model that tracks a human-style suspicion state may fit them poorly; a model that tracks task progress and context contents may fit better.
- Planted artefacts and prompt-style lures have delayed effects (the agent acts on them several steps later), which is a credit-assignment problem a one-step reward cannot express. AgentSnare's stated motivation is exactly the multi-step failure of static lures.
- Attacker type (script, human, LLM agent) is latent and changes which response is best. Reworr and Volkov's pipeline is in effect an active type-identification probe, which is a POMDP information-gathering action rather than a reward-maximising one.
- Time sensitivity: the "8 in 8.1 million" figure is from 2024. LLM capability and prevalence results from 2024 and 2025 should be treated as lower bounds as of November 2026.

### Gaps
- No published study found on whether LLM attackers adapt to a decoy's response policy across sessions (for example through memory, fine-tuning or operator feedback).
- No peer-reviewed in-the-wild prevalence measurement of LLM-driven attackers on honeypots after early 2025 was found.
- All 2026 items are abstract-level and unreviewed as far as I could see; a search listing placed Honeyval at ICML 2026 but I did not open that page.
- I did not open "Towards Agentic Honeynet Configuration" (arXiv:2603.14122) or "HoneyTrap" (arXiv:2601.04034), which appeared in searches; they are not used above.

## 4. Autonomous cyber defence benchmarks with decoys (CAGE, CybORG and related): how do they model the adversary, and do learned defenders beat scripted ones?

### Takeaway
CAGE Challenge 2 is a partially observable environment with fixed rule-based red agents; learned defenders beat the one scripted baseline by a wide margin, but the winner hard-coded its decoy placement, policies specialised to each scripted attacker, and performance fell against an unseen attack strategy. In CAGE Challenge 4, where red can probe for decoys, engineered heuristics reportedly beat the submitted multi-agent learners, though I could only confirm that second-hand.

### Cited Findings

**CAGE Challenge 2 (CybORG)**
- Formal model: "a partially observable stochastic environment that is described by the tuple ⟨I, S, A, O, T, R_i⟩"; blue has 145 discrete actions in three classes: reconnaissance (Monitor, Analyse), deception (decoy services) and restorative (Remove, Restore). [FULL] — [Kiely, Bowman, Standen, Moir 2023, 2nd Int. Workshop on Adaptive Cyber Defence, arXiv:2309.07388](https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.07388)
- Adversary: "three unique rules-based Red agents": B-line (has prior knowledge of the network and takes the shortest path to the operational server), Meander (no prior knowledge, explores subnet by subnet) and Sleep (no actions). Both active agents pick the top-ranked available exploit 75% of the time and a random other one 25% of the time. [FULL] — [Kiely et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.07388); [CAGE Challenge 2 repository](https://github.com/cage-challenge/cage-challenge-2)
- Decoys: any exploit against a decoy service fails automatically and the access is a clear indicator of red activity (green agents never touch decoys). Setting a decoy costs blue one timestep "whereas the Red agent requires a minimum of two timesteps, in most cases, to realise it is dealing with a decoy". The scripted red does not reason about decoys: it ends up "continuously exploiting fake ports within a host". [FULL] — [CAGE Challenge 2 repository](https://github.com/cage-challenge/cage-challenge-2); [Kiely et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.07388)
- Results: the final leaderboard is led by "PPO + Greedy Decoys" (CardiffUni, total −54.57 ± 0.43), followed by ensembles and hierarchical PPO variants (about −57). The only rule-based defender listed, "CCS Heuristic: Blue React Restore", scored −823.89; random −2015.61; sleeping −3112.2. The winning entry combined PPO with a greedy decoy heuristic. [FULL] — [CAGE Challenge 2 repository](https://github.com/cage-challenge/cage-challenge-2)
- The only non-deep-learning submission (Monte Carlo tree search) "scored the lowest" and could not finish evaluation in time. [FULL] — [Kiely et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.07388)
- Strategy analysis: top agents "learnt vastly different strategies" with similar reward: two deployed many decoys, one mostly analysed and removed. The same agent used different action mixes against different red agents (many Femitter decoys against B-line, a broad sweep against Meander). Shorter episodes used more decoys; longer ones relied more on removal. [FULL] — [Kiely et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.07388)
- Specialisation against generality: training against one attacker gives a policy "only effective in countering that attacker, while performing poorly against other attackers"; training against all gives a general policy that is inferior to the specialists. Hierarchical agents, the best class, select a specialised sub-policy per red agent. [FULL] — [Kiely et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.07388)
- Generalisation: a team that placed second reports "In unseen environments, all of our approaches perform worse", and against a new attack strategy "our models had reduced overall performance even though the new strategy was less efficient". [ABS] — [Wolk, Applebaum, Dennler, Dwyer, Moskowitz, Nguyen, Nichols, Park, Rachwalski, Rau, Webster 2022, NeurIPS RL for Real Life workshop, arXiv:2211.15557](https://arxiv.org/abs/2211.15557)
- Planning with a model: a formal causal model of CAGE-2 plus online tree search (C-POMCP) "achieves state-of-the-art performance" and is "two orders of magnitude more efficient in computing time" than the closest competitor; the authors note that leading methods are model-free offline reinforcement learning without optimality guarantees. [ABS] — [Hammar, Dhir, Stadler 2024, arXiv:2407.11070](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.11070)

**CAGE Challenge 4**
- Multi-agent blue team; evaluation is against a `FiniteStateRedAgent` over 100 episodes of 500 steps; red agents "will each use a randomly selected strategy". Red's action list includes `DiscoverDeception`, which probes a host for decoys with a stated 50% false-negative and 10% false-positive rate, and a withdraw action. Attacking a decoy alerts blue and the exploit fails. [FULL] — [CAGE Challenge 4 repository](https://github.com/cage-challenge/cage-challenge-4)
- The challenge papers present it as a test of whether multi-agent reinforcement learning is a robust and scalable approach to network defence. [ABS] — [Kiely et al. 2025, "Exploring the Efficacy of Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning for Autonomous Cyber Defence: A CAGE Challenge 4 Perspective", AAAI 39(28):28907-28913, DOI 10.1609/aaai.v39i28.35158](https://mlanthology.org/aaai/2025/kiely2025aaai-exploring); [Kiely et al. 2025, AI Magazine 46(3):e70021](https://doi.org/10.1002/aaai.70021)
- Outcome, second-hand: a June 2026 preprint states that in the competition "carefully engineered heuristics outperformed the submitted multi-agent reinforcement learning (MARL) agents", giving a best heuristic top-team return of −113 ± 35 against −193 ± 84 for the top MARL entry, citing Kiely et al. 2025. It describes the default red as a scripted finite-state process that "selects targets probabilistically using a fixed host-state transition matrix". [2ND for the competition result: I read it in this preprint, not in the Kiely papers or leaderboard, which I could not open] — [Silva 2026, arXiv:2606.13832](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.13832)
- A graph-based approach frames the task as a two-player, context-based partially observable Markov decision problem (paraphrase of the abstract) and claims zero-shot adaptation to unseen networks and a wide margin over prior learned methods. [ABS] — [King, Bowman, Huang 2025, arXiv:2509.16151](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.16151)

**Related simulator**
- Honeypots and decoys were added to Microsoft's CyberBattleSim and tested against reinforcement-learning attackers; attacker progress depended on the number and placement of deceptive elements. [ABS] — [Walter, Ferguson-Walter, Ridley 2021, 1st Int. Workshop on Adaptive Cyber Defense, arXiv:2108.13980](https://arxiv.org/abs/2108.13980)

### Inferences
- The benchmark designers model the defender's problem as partially observable and sequential even when the adversary is a fixed script. None of the CAGE formulations is a bandit.
- "Learned beats scripted" in CAGE-2 is a weak claim: the scripted baseline is a single reactive rule, the red agents are fixed and do not model decoys, and the winner's decoy logic was itself hand-coded. Where red can probe for deception (CAGE-4), the reported result reverses. The evidence that learning adds value specifically in deciding when and where to deceive is thin.
- The CAGE-2 specialisation finding (a policy per attacker, selected by a controller) matches a latent-attacker-type structure: identify the type, then apply a type-specific policy. That is a POMDP over attacker type in practice. One leaderboard entry is named "HIPPO Bandit Controller", which suggests a bandit used at the sub-policy selection level; the repository page gives no design details.
- Training against scripted attackers produced policies that degraded against an unseen strategy. By analogy, a policy learned offline from logs of scripted attackers should not be expected to transfer to humans or LLM agents, and failing to beat rules on those same scripts says little about its value against adaptive attackers.
- Decoys in these simulators are placement actions with a simple effect (exploit fails, alert raised). They do not model response content, so these benchmarks offer no evidence about per-response policies.

### Gaps
- I could not open the CAGE-4 leaderboard or the full Kiely et al. 2025 papers (AAAI site returned an access-denied page; Wiley returned HTTP 403). The heuristics-beat-MARL statement needs checking against the primary source.
- The original CybORG paper (Standen et al. 2021) and CAGE Challenges 1 and 3 were not reviewed.
- No benchmark was found with an adaptive, learning red agent that reasons about decoy response content, or with human or LLM red agents in the loop at scale.
- Minor inconsistency in the CAGE-2 paper: it names eight decoy actions but later refers to "seven decoy types"; the repository lists seven.

## 5. When is a one-step (bandit) formulation adequate, and when is a sequential or adversarial one required? Non-stationary or adversarial bandits in security.

### Takeaway
The literature uses bandits for repeated, session-independent choices such as which honeypot configuration to deploy, and uses MDP, POMDP or game models whenever the defender responds inside an interaction. Theory says ordinary regret guarantees are meaningless against an adversary that adapts to the learner, and no algorithm achieves sublinear policy regret against an adaptive adversary with unbounded memory. A bandit is adequate for a response policy only where the attacker's next input does not depend on the response, which holds for open-loop scripts and fails for humans and LLM agents.

### Cited Findings

**Bandits as used in security**
- Honeypot configuration selection as a combinatorial multi-armed bandit: each round the defender picks which honeypot configurations to deploy and the attacker picks an exploit; exploit values change over time. Defenders tested were UCB (stochastic) and EXP3 (adversarial) against uniform random, fixed random and pure strategies, all against an "Adversarial Attacker, modeled after fictitious play". The authors note "our model does not map directly to a stochastic problem, nor a classical adversarial problem". EXP3 and UCB outperformed the naive defenders in cumulative regret over 20,000 rounds. It is a two-page extended abstract with simulation only. [FULL] — [Gutierrez and Kiekintveld 2017, AAMAS, pp. 1565-1567](https://ifaamas.csc.liv.ac.uk/Proceedings/aamas2017/pdfs/p1565.pdf)
- The same paper describes Klíma, Lisý and Kiekintveld (GameSec 2015) as modelling "an online, repeated interaction between a defender and attacker" in border patrol with a combinatorial bandit, evaluated against "an attacker agent derived from fictitious play". [2ND, via Gutierrez and Kiekintveld] — [Gutierrez and Kiekintveld 2017](https://ifaamas.csc.liv.ac.uk/Proceedings/aamas2017/pdfs/p1565.pdf)
- Xu, Tran-Thanh and Jennings (AAMAS 2016), "Playing Repeated Security Games with No Prior Knowledge": an adversarial online-learning defender with low regret against the best fixed strategy in hindsight when payoffs and attacker behaviour are unknown. [2ND, search summary only; the repository returned HTTP 403] — [University of Southampton record](https://eprints.soton.ac.uk/387256/)
- Human attackers against bandit-style defenders: static defences are learned quickly; an adaptive bandit defender is hardest for humans to learn, with its advantage appearing after about 20 rounds. [FULL] — [Gutierrez et al. 2019, CogSci](https://escholarship.org/uc/item/2gn3w566)

**Theory on adaptive adversaries**
- Standard regret becomes inadequate when the adversary can adapt to the learner's actions; the authors define policy regret instead. No bandit algorithm can guarantee sublinear policy regret against an adaptive adversary with unbounded memory; with bounded memory (all paraphrased from the abstract), any low-regret bandit algorithm can be converted into one with sublinear policy regret. [ABS] — [Arora, Dekel, Tewari 2012, ICML, arXiv:1206.6400](https://arxiv.org/abs/1206.6400)

**Sequential formulations used for in-interaction responses**
- Taxonomy-level statement that dynamic models such as POSGs are "necessary to effectively study attacker engagement". [FULL] — [Pawlick et al. 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1712.05441)
- Per-command allow or block with an attacker who retries, switches or quits: probabilistic automaton plus game. [FULL] — [Wagener et al. 2009](https://sos-vo.org/system/files/sos_files/Self_Adaptive_High_Interaction_Honeypots_Driven_by_Game_Theory.pdf)
- Honeypot response to botmaster commands: MDP, then POMDP for uncertainty about what the botmaster believes. [ABS] — [Hayatle et al. 2013](https://doi.org/10.1080/19393555.2013.828802)
- Honeynet engagement: semi-Markov decision process with reinforcement learning. [ABS] — [Huang and Zhu 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1906.12182)
- IoT honeypot response selection: attacker behaviour modelled as an MDP, with the response policy learned by reinforcement learning from attack traces collected on real devices; deployed on the Internet. [ABS] — [Guan, Liu, Cao, Zhu, La Porta 2023, WiSec, arXiv:2305.06430](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.06430)
- Engage or block after detection, with attacker belief as state: one-sided POSG. [FULL] — [Horák et al. 2017](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10066860)
- Intrusion response against an attacker that adapts to the defender's strategy: stopping game solved by self-play. [ABS] — [Hammar and Stadler 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2301.06085)
- Benchmarks: CAGE-2 specified as a partially observable stochastic environment even with scripted red agents. [FULL] — [Kiely et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.07388)

### Inferences

**What each model assumes and what it would mean for a response policy.** This table is my synthesis; the right-hand column names the cited work that instantiates each row.

| Formulation | Assumes about the attacker and interaction | Adequate when | Consequence for policy design | Instances in the literature |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Contextual bandit (stochastic) | Next input is independent of the response; reward is immediate; attacker behaviour is stationary and ignores the policy | Attacker is an open-loop script; or one decision per independent session; or the choice is made across sessions (which configuration to expose) | Per-input reward model; offline learning from logs is valid; cannot value delay, information leakage or planted artefacts | Gutierrez and Kiekintveld 2017 (configuration choice, UCB) |
| Adversarial or non-stationary bandit | Rewards may be chosen adversarially, but only meaningful if the adversary is oblivious or has bounded memory | Attacker population drifts; attacker adapts slowly relative to rounds | Randomised policy (EXP3-style); guarantees are against the best fixed action in hindsight, not the best policy | Gutierrez and Kiekintveld 2017 (EXP3); Xu et al. 2016 [2ND]; limits in Arora et al. 2012 |
| MDP | Response changes what the attacker does next; attacker follows a fixed stochastic policy; state is observable | Scripted but closed-loop attackers (branch on output, retry, quit) | Optimise session-level return; needs a transition model or interactive data; offline logs must cover counterfactual responses | Wagener et al. 2009; Hayatle et al. 2013; Huang and Zhu 2019 (semi-Markov); HoneyIoT 2023 |
| POMDP | As MDP, plus hidden state: attacker type, goal, and suspicion | Mixed population of scripts, humans and LLM agents; suspicion matters | Maintain a belief; some responses are probes chosen for information; type-specific sub-policies under a controller | Hayatle et al. 2013 (extension); CAGE-2 (Kiely et al. 2023; Hammar et al. 2024) |
| One-sided POSG / stochastic game | Attacker is strategic and adapts to the defender's policy; attacker's belief about deception is part of the state | Skilled humans or agents who reason about being deceived; repeated exposure to the same policy | Mixed strategies; control what each response reveals; worst-case guarantees; computationally hard beyond small models | Horák et al. 2017, 2019; Hammar and Stadler 2024; Pawlick et al. 2019 recommendation |
| Hypergame | Attacker plays a different game from the real one and may never learn it | Deception that works by sustained misperception (decoys, honeytokens, sandbox escalation) | Design to keep the attacker's perceived game consistent; fidelity and consistency become constraints | Kulkarni and Fu 2020; Wan et al. 2021 |

- A bandit is adequate for a response policy under two testable conditions: the attacker's next input is statistically independent of the response given the context, and the value of a response is realised immediately. Both are empirical properties of the attacker population, and both can be tested on logged sessions by checking whether the next-input distribution differs by response.
- A bandit trained on scripted attackers failing to beat hand-written rules is consistent with both readings: if the scripts are open-loop, responses barely change outcomes and there is little for any learner to gain; if they are closed-loop, the bandit optimises the wrong objective. The result does not show that learning has no value, only that this formulation on this attacker class has none.
- For humans and LLM agents the evidence in sections 2 and 3 contradicts both bandit assumptions: responses change subsequent behaviour (hunting, redirection by planted content), and benefits are delayed (time wasted, later use of a planted artefact).
- Response types differ in how sequential they are. Delay and a single fake error against a script are close to one-step. Fake success commits the decoy to a consistent fiction for the rest of the session. Planting a honeytoken pays off only later, possibly in another session. Escalation to a real sandbox is a near-irreversible state change. The richer the action set, the less a one-step model fits.
- Adaptation operates at two timescales. Within a session the issue is state (MDP or POMDP). Across sessions the issue is the attacker learning the policy (game). Arora et al.'s result implies that adversarial-bandit algorithms do not rescue the within-session problem, because a reactive attacker is an adaptive adversary with memory.
- A layered design is consistent with the literature: a bandit or fixed rules for traffic classified as open-loop scripts, a belief over attacker type updated from behaviour (including deliberate probes), and a sequential policy with randomisation for sessions classified as human or LLM-driven. CAGE-2's hierarchical winners and the type-detection honeypot of Reworr and Volkov are partial precedents. This is a design inference, not something any single source demonstrates.
- Offline training from lab logs of scripted attackers cannot identify the effect of a response on future attacker behaviour for attacker classes absent from the logs. The CAGE generalisation results (Wolk et al.) support the same caution in a neighbouring setting.

### Gaps
- I found no paper that formulates per-input honeypot response selection as a contextual bandit and tests the non-adaptivity assumption, and no head-to-head comparison of bandit, MDP and game formulations on the same honeypot data.
- No result was found on non-stationary bandits (drifting or switching) applied specifically to honeypot response; the security bandit literature I found concerns configuration or patrol choice across rounds.
- I did not open Xu et al. 2016, Klíma et al. 2015, or Balcan et al. 2015 ("Commitment Without Regrets"); they should be read before being cited as more than pointers.
- Reinforcement-learning honeypots that predate HoneyIoT (for example Heliza, RASSH, QRASSH) were out of scope for this note and not opened; they are relevant to whether the MDP formulation has beaten static honeypots in practice.
- Whether real LLM attack agents retain memory of a decoy's behaviour across sessions, which decides between a POMDP and a game for that class, is not addressed in anything I found.
